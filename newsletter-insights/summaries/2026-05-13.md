### High Performance Rate Limiting at Databricks `Engineering`
- The original Databricks rate limiter relied on a single Redis instance with two network hops per request; under P99 cloud network latency of 10–20ms, this dominated every rate-limit decision that should have taken microseconds
- Real-time model serving was the forcing function: a single customer could now generate orders-of-magnitude more traffic than the original service was designed for, exposing three failure modes: tail latency, horizontal scaling ceiling, and a single point of failure
- Consistent hashing with Envoy (their first fix attempt) hit hard limits: non-Envoy services couldn't participate, hash churn on scale events forced Redis syncs, and hotspotting saturated single nodes while neighbors sat idle — adding machines stopped helping
- The key architectural insight: rate limit counts are transient (per-second window), so durable storage is over-engineering; in-memory counters on the owning server are sufficient and dramatically faster
- **Dicer** is their internal routing layer that solves the hard part: it lets any server keep state in-memory while remaining horizontally scalable and fault-tolerant — clients query Dicer for key ownership, then go directly to the authoritative replica
- Moving counters in-memory eliminated the Redis network hop entirely; tail latency dropped sharply, horizontal scaling resumed linear behavior, and the single point of failure disappeared because each replica is authoritative for its own key slice
- The explicit accuracy tradeoff: strict counting is expensive at scale; Databricks traded count precision for a faster critical path — the rate limiter answers "as if the decision has already been made" rather than waiting for a perfectly consistent count
- Takeaway for system designers: when you hit a scaling ceiling where adding resources doesn't improve latency, the architecture itself is often the bottleneck — not under-provisioning

### You Just Respawn: The Most Freeing Reframe About Failure `Other`
- Core framework: failure in life maps to the "respawn" mechanic in video games — you don't stop playing, you restart with more information and experience than the previous run
- Silicon Valley reframe from a founder conversation: when the author described a failed prior attempt at an idea, the response was "That's actually great. That means you know what not to do." — positions failure history as compounding knowledge, not sunk cost
- Counterintuitive claim: "One of the best ways to increase your success rate is to increase your failure rate" — because each failure is a data point narrowing the solution space
- The hockey team analogy: Stanley Cup winners aren't teams that never lost — they're teams with consistent belief and grit that kept showing up through losses; positions consistency-under-adversity above avoiding failure altogether
- Behavioral implication: holding back due to fear of being seen trying is a worse outcome than failing publicly, because "the ones who are watching are inspired by people who try"
- The framing collapses the binary of success/failure into a single continuous process: the only losing condition is stopping entirely
