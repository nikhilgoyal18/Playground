## Product Coalition (Jay Stansell)
*1 issue this period*

### The Hardest Product Decision: When to Build for Teams `Product`
- The Kano Model explains why adding team features often kills individual products: individual UX creates "attractive quality" (delight), while team features like admin panels and permissions are "must-be" qualities — they prevent dissatisfaction but generate none. If the must-be features degrade the attractive quality, you've traded the reason people cared for infrastructure nobody loves.
- First team release (v0.6, October 2025) shipped with flat-list team structure: owner invites members via email, sees aggregate analytics, manages billing via Stripe. Within weeks, it exposed a "conceptual model mismatch" (Don Norman): the product modeled teams as flat lists; users expected overlapping, hierarchical, fluid org structures.
- Privacy problem specific to vulnerable-use products: when users practice high-stakes conversations (board presentations, salary negotiations), they receive intimate feedback (confidence score, filler word count). Kahneman and Tversky's loss aversion says the fear of a manager seeing a bad session (~2x stronger than gain motivation) causes users to stop practicing or only rehearse what they're already good at.
- Solution — aggregation not transparency: managers see trends (e.g., "EPS improved 15% over past month"), not transcripts. Engagement patterns visible but not content. Individual owns detailed data; team sees trajectory only.
- Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory as design constraint: people need autonomy, competence, and relatedness to stay intrinsically motivated. Full transparency to managers kills autonomy. The aggregation model preserves autonomy by giving members control over what and when they practice.
- v2.1 rebuild (March 2026) tackled team formation UX. Previous pattern was top-down: admin buys seats, manually types every email. Viewed through BJ Fogg's B=MAP: each email typed is friction that reduces Ability. Observed result: admins who bought 10 seats would invite 3 people, get distracted, and never finish.
- Implication for any B2B product: the enterprise buyer (who needs ROI and aggregate visibility) and the individual user (who needs psychological safety) have structurally opposed needs. Solving this requires separate data contracts, not a permissions toggle.

### EP212: Data Warehouse vs Data Lake vs Data Mesh `Engineering`
- **Data Warehouse**: structures and cleans data before storing it; fast queries and consistent reports; adding new sources requires schema conformance upfront — high governance, low flexibility.
- **Data Lake**: stores everything raw (databases, logs, images, video); processes data at query time; if naming, formatting, and ownership rules aren't enforced, results in duplicate, outdated, and undocumented data ("data swamp").
- **Data Mesh**: shifts data ownership from a central team to individual departments (sales owns sales data, finance owns finance data); shared standards keep cross-team data compatible; works at scale but requires every team to staff and maintain their own data quality and documentation.
- In practice, companies typically combine all three: warehouse for dashboards/reporting, lake for ML workloads, and mesh principles as teams scale.
- **Polling vs Long Polling vs Webhooks vs SSE** compared: Polling sends a request every N seconds regardless of new data (simplest, wasteful); Long Polling holds the HTTP connection open until data arrives or timeout (fewer empty responses, used in legacy chat apps); SSE opens a persistent HTTP connection for server-to-client streaming (one-way, no overhead — how AI token-by-token responses are delivered); Webhooks invert the model — service POSTs to your callback URL on event (e.g., Stripe payment events).
- **API design pitfalls beyond basic HTTP**: naming, pagination, versioning, and backward compatibility are often underspecified early and expensive to fix later; idempotency, rate limits, and retry semantics are ignored until production load exposes them.
- **API security layers**: API keys are entry-level; OAuth + JWTs + scopes are the production standard; mistakes here are "costly" — the newsletter flags this as an area where weak early decisions compound.
- Supporting infrastructure for API growth: clear documentation, OpenAPI specs, observability, and contract testing allow teams to trust and use the API without reverse-engineering its behavior.
