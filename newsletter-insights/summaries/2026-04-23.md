## ByteByteGo
*1 issue this period*

### B-Trees vs LSM Trees: Comparison and Trade-Offs `Engineering`
- The fundamental database storage problem: data lives on disk, and every read/write eventually reaches disk — how data is organized on disk determines all performance characteristics
- B-Trees keep data sorted on disk so reads are fast, but pay for it on every write (write amplification)
- LSM Trees (Log-Structured Merge-Trees) buffer writes in memory and flush to disk in bulk — writes are cheap but reads are more expensive (read amplification, compaction overhead)
- Neither is better — they represent fundamentally different approaches optimized for opposite workloads; understanding the tradeoff is one of the most useful mental models in system design

## Jay Stansell (Product Coalition)
*1 issue this period*

### Prioritization frameworks are just opinion laundering `Product`
- 2017 survey of 50 PMs (Kate Bennet, Product Coalition): in 46% of companies, leadership or the head of product decides what gets built next; only 13% of PMs have the actual authority to decide themselves
- Gut feel and CEO preference were each cited by ~43% of respondents as inputs to what gets built — meaning product management as a profession largely invented tooling to rationalize decisions already made elsewhere
- Jordan Lamborn reviewed 100+ prioritization frameworks and found nearly all share the same blind spots: "some type of value is the most prevalent factor" — impact vs. effort, wearing different hats; variables that actually matter (customer centricity, hypothesis confidence, strategic alignment) barely show up
- WSJF and similar frameworks miss the downside entirely — each new feature creates new security surface, maintenance burden, and complexity that compounds quietly: "also a new risk is created" (Hans-Jörg Roser)
- Core critique: *"If the CEO is still picking what gets built, no RICE score will save you. You are just laundering someone else's opinion through a spreadsheet."*
- The reframe that changes the conversation: treat prioritization as capital allocation, not feature ranking — each roadmap item has an expected return, a risk profile, and a maintenance cost extending years past launch
- Stop asking "what should we build next" → start asking "where should we invest, and what is the cost of being wrong"
- Caio Flores: "It's easy to think that the lack of features is causing your startup to not grow enough, but remember that you have an infinite number of variables that could be causing that" — wrong market, wrong timing, wrong value proposition are not fixable by shipping more features
