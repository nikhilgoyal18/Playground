### EP211: How the JVM Works `Engineering`
- javac compiles source code into platform-independent bytecode stored as .class files, JARs, or modules
- Class loading uses parent delegation: Bootstrap handles core JDK classes, Platform covers extensions, System loads application code
- Bytecode linking has three steps: Verify (checks bytecode safety), Prepare (allocates static fields with default values), Resolve (converts symbolic references to direct memory addresses)
- JVM memory is shared (Heap, Method Area) across threads; per-thread resources are JVM stack, PC register, and native method stack
- Execution combines interpretation and JIT compilation: interpreter runs bytecode directly; when a method is called multiple times, JIT compiler converts it to native machine code stored in the code cache

### The pause that creates doubt in behavioral interviews `Other`
- A hesitation when asked "can you imagine doing this again at my company?" signals missing repeatable thinking — the interviewer heard "this person found something valuable once," not "this person has a system"
- Version 1 (what you did): "I pulled delinquent accounts data and segmented by payment method…" — success looks like a one-time event
- Version 2 (how you think): "When I'm looking for missed opportunities, I always segment first because the problem space is never one homogeneous group. Different segments usually have different root causes…" — success looks repeatable
- "Without the thinking, your success looks like a one-time event. With the thinking, your success looks repeatable."
- Self-check after telling any story: ask "did I explain how I think about this type of problem?" — if someone could take your approach and apply it to a different problem, your thinking is visible
- The $7M example: segmenting delinquent accounts by payment method and review status revealed that 50% were high-risk accounts driving up costs — the insight came from the segmentation mental model, not luck

### The story isn't the problem—the framing is `Other`
- Interview questions aren't asking for the same thing — they ask who you are through different lenses: "What made you proud?" vs "What was most impactful?" vs "What was the hardest problem?" require different emphases on the same story
- "Matching the question type matters more than sounding impressive" — a beautifully structured story that misses the question leaves the interviewer doing too much cognitive work to find the point
- Cognitive load principle: if the answer is cognitively heavy, too indirect, or mismatched, interviewers won't feel emotional resonance — "no spark of recognition, no 'this is the kind of leader we need'"
- Framework — same project, four different apertures:
  - **Proud story**: "Here's what I value. Here's how this project let me live that value."
  - **Impact story**: "Here's the scale of the problem. Here's the scale of change I created."
  - **Hardest-problem story**: "Here's why this was complex for me. Here's how I broke it down."
  - **Conflict story**: "Here's the disagreement. Here's why it triggered emotions. Here's how I made the case while taking care of people's feelings."
- "AI skips this too unless you specifically teach them how to think clearly and deeply" — LLMs default to "make it sound more senior" without checking whether the story answers what was actually asked
- When framing is correct, candidates stop sounding rehearsed and start sounding intentional — leadership hears the person behind the story, not just a sequence of events

### I had a million followers in my journal before I had them in real life `Other`
- Writing goals in present tense as stated fact ("I have a million followers") vs. aspirational framing ("I want…") is a deliberate brain-training technique, not self-delusion
- Core neuroscience principle: the brain prefers familiarity over possibility — wired for survival, it registers the unfamiliar big dream as a threat, not an opportunity
- Self-sabotage before breakthroughs isn't weakness or lack of discipline — it's the brain doing its job by pulling you back toward what feels safe and known
- Mechanism to override it: daily repetition through writing, visualization, and journaling as if the goal has already happened literally familiarizes the brain with that reality — "you are making the unfamiliar feel familiar"
- Once familiar, the brain stops treating the dream as a threat and starts working toward it — shifts from "is this possible?" to "how do I make this happen?"
- "Your brain will work to confirm whatever you believe — so give it something worth confirming"
- Practical application: write the goal that feels too big in present tense every day until your nervous system treats it as expected, not exceptional
